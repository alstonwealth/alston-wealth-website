Last edition covered how married couples should think about Social Security. This one steps back to the regrets I hear most often from people who are a few years into retirement.

Almost none of them are about not saving enough. They're about decisions made in the first few years, often by good savers who did almost everything else right. The full list of seven mistakes is on our site. Here are the regrets behind them.

The regrets I hear most

  1. "I knew my balance, not my paycheck." Retirement runs on monthly income: how much comes in, where it comes from and how long it lasts.
  2. "My taxes jumped in my 70s." Spending savings first and leaving the untaxed account alone feels smart, until required withdrawals start at 73 or 75.
  3. "I sold low in year one." With everything in the market, a bad first year forces you to sell to pay the bills. Two to three years in safe money prevents that.
  4. "Nobody told me about the Medicare penalty." Miss the sign-up window at 65 without other coverage and the penalty can last for life.
  5. "The form was never updated." The beneficiary form on each account, not the will, decides who gets the money.
  6. "We planned for two of us, not for one." When a spouse dies, one Social Security check stops and the survivor files taxes as a single person, with brackets about half the size.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common retirement mistake?
Not having a plan for monthly income. Many people know their balance but not what it pays them each month, or which account the money should come from first.

How much should I keep in safe money when I retire?
A common starting point is two to three years of the withdrawals you expect to take, so a market drop doesn't force you to sell investments at a low point.

Does my will override my beneficiary forms?
No. For retirement accounts, the beneficiary form on file with each account usually decides who gets the money.

What is the widow's tax?
After one spouse dies, the survivor files as single, with tax brackets about half the size, so taxes often go up even though household income went down.
