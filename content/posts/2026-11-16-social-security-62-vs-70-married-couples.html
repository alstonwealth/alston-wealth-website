The same person can get $1,750 a month from Social Security, or $2,500, or $3,100, for the rest of their life. The only thing that changes is the age they claim.

Here's the math on 62 vs 67 vs 70, when waiting actually pays off, and the part married couples almost always miss.

The three ages

If you were born in 1960 or later, your full retirement age is 67. That's the age where you get your full benefit. Say that full amount is $2,500 a month.

  • Claim at 62, the earliest you can, and you get about 30% less: about $1,750 a month. That cut is permanent. It doesn't go back up when you turn 67.
  • Claim at 67 and you get the full $2,500.
  • Wait past 67 and every year adds 8%, for life. Wait all the way to 70 and you get about 24% more: about $3,100 a month.

On top of that, each of those checks gets the yearly cost of living raise. Same person, same work history, three very different paychecks.

When does waiting pay off?

This is the question everyone asks. If you compare claiming at 62 with claiming at 67, the person who waited catches up in total dollars around age 79. Before that, the early claimer is ahead. After that, the person who waited pulls ahead and stays ahead every year after.

Compare 62 with 70, and the break-even is around age 80 or 81.

A lot of people stop there. "I don't know if I'll make it to 81," they say, and they take it at 62. If you're single, that's a fair way to think about it. If you're married, it's the wrong question.

Why married couples are different

When one spouse passes away, the survivor doesn't keep both Social Security checks. The smaller check goes away, and the survivor keeps the bigger one.

So the higher earner's check isn't just their check. It's the check the survivor will live on, maybe for 10 or 20 years after the first spouse is gone.

That changes the question. It's not "Will I live to 81?" It's "Will either of us live to 81?" And the odds that at least one of you makes it into your late 80s are a lot better than the odds for just one person. That's why, for the higher earner, waiting often matters more than people think. You're buying a bigger check for whichever one of you lives longer.

The lower earner and the spousal benefit

The lower earner may be able to get a spousal benefit of up to half of the other spouse's full-age amount, if that's bigger than their own. But here's the catch most people don't know: the spousal benefit doesn't grow after your full retirement age. There's no 8% a year for waiting. So for a lot of lower earners, there's no reason to wait past 67, and sometimes it makes sense to claim earlier.

When claiming at 62 can make sense

  • Your health gives you real reason to expect a shorter life, and you're single.
  • You don't have savings to cover the gap. Waiting only works if you have something else to live on.
  • You're the lower earner in a couple, and the higher earner is waiting.
  • You simply need the money. That's a real answer, and there's no shame in it.

One warning: if you claim before your full retirement age while you're still working and you earn over a certain limit, Social Security holds back part of your check until you reach full retirement age. Claiming early while you're still working often backfires.

The tax bonus of waiting

Here's the part almost no one connects. If you wait on Social Security and live on your savings in your 60s, you can draw from the account you haven't paid tax on yet while you're in low tax brackets, before the required withdrawals start at 73 or 75. What a lot of couples end up doing

The lower earner claims earlier, sometimes at 62, sometimes at their full age. The higher earner waits as long as the budget comfortably allows, often to 70. You get some income coming in now, and you lock in the biggest possible check for the survivor.
