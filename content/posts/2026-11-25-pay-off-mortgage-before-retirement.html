Almost everyone I sit down with grew up hearing the same thing: retire with the house paid off. Be debt free. For a lot of people, that's exactly right.

But I've also watched people do it the wrong way and hand tens of thousands of dollars to taxes for no reason. The difference usually isn't whether you pay it off. It's where the money comes from, and when.

The good reasons to pay it off

They're real. When the house is paid off, your biggest bill disappears. If your payment is $1,500 a month, that's $18,000 a year you no longer have to pull from savings. Less money coming out means less tax, and your savings have less work to do.

And honestly, a lot of people just sleep better. That counts. Peace of mind is a big part of why you saved in the first place.

Mistake 1: Paying it off from the account you haven't paid tax on yet

Say you owe $150,000 and decide to wipe it out the year you retire. Most of your money is in your retirement account at work, the one you've never paid tax on, so that's where you pull from.

The problem is that every dollar coming out of that account counts as income. To end up with $150,000 after taxes, you might have to take out $200,000 or more. And because it all lands in one year, a big piece of it gets taxed at your highest rate.

It gets worse. Medicare looks at your income from two years earlier to set your premiums, so one big withdrawal can raise what you pay for Medicare two years later. You paid off a loan at 3% by setting off a tax bill that can eat 20 to 30 cents of every dollar.

Mistake 2: Locking up your cash

Once money goes into the house, it's hard to get back out. You'd have to sell or borrow against it, and borrowing gets harder once you're retired with no paycheck.

I've seen people pay off the house, then get hit with a health problem, a new roof, or a kid who needs help. Now they're stuck pulling more out of their retirement accounts, sometimes in a bad market. You can't pay a hospital bill with a paid-off kitchen.

Mistake 3: Ignoring the rate

If you locked in a mortgage at 3%, that's very cheap money. If your safe money is earning more than that, there's no rush to pay it off. You're coming out ahead by keeping the loan, even if having debt bothers you.

If your rate is 7%, it's a different story. Paying it down is like earning a guaranteed 7%, and that's hard to beat anywhere. In that case, paying it off often makes a lot of sense.

The tax deduction myth

A lot of people still believe they need the mortgage for the tax deduction. For most retirees, that's not true anymore. Most people take the standard deduction now, so the mortgage interest doesn't save them a dime in taxes. Don't keep a mortgage just for the deduction.

When paying it off makes sense

  • Your rate is high.
  • You have cash beyond your emergency money and two to three years of safe money.
  • You can pay it from money that's already been taxed, like a savings account or a regular investment account, instead of an account where every withdrawal counts as income.

The middle path

If most of your money is in the untaxed account, you don't have to choose all or nothing:

  • Pay a little extra every month instead of one big lump sum.
  • Spread the payoff over a few years, especially in the low-tax years between when you stop working and when Social Security and the required withdrawals start. More on that in which account to take money from first.
  • Keep a payoff fund. This is what a lot of my clients do. Set the money aside in savings earning interest, knowing you could pay the house off any day you want. You get most of the peace of mind and you keep your options open.

The takeaway

A paid-off house is a great feeling, and the feeling matters. But do the math first: what's your rate, where's the money coming from, and what will it cost in taxes to get it out?
