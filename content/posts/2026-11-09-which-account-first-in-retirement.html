Most people take money out of their retirement accounts in the order that feels right. For a lot of them, that order quietly costs tens of thousands of dollars in taxes over their retirement.

Here's which account to take money from first, why the most common advice can backfire, and what usually works better.

The three kinds of money in retirement

  1. Regular savings and investments. Your bank account, a brokerage account: money you've already paid tax on. When you spend it, you mostly don't owe tax, except on any growth.
  2. Money you haven't paid tax on yet. The plan from your old job, or the individual retirement account you moved it into. Every dollar you take out counts as income, and you pay tax on it.
  3. A Roth account. You paid the tax going in, so it grows tax-free and comes out tax-free.

The common advice, and why it backfires

The advice most people hear is simple: spend your savings first, then the account you haven't paid tax on, and save the Roth for last so the tax-free money grows as long as possible.

For the first few years, it looks great. You pay almost no tax, because you're living on money you've already paid tax on. But the untaxed account just sits there and grows, and it doesn't stay untaxed forever.

At 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later, the government makes you start pulling money out of it every year. Those required withdrawals get bigger as the account gets bigger. Stack Social Security on top, and your income jumps, your tax bracket jumps, and because Medicare sets your premiums from your income, those can go up too.

Then there's the part almost nobody plans for. If you're married, eventually one of you passes away. The survivor keeps most of the income but now files taxes as a single person, with tax brackets about half the size. Same money, much bigger tax bill.

So you didn't avoid the tax. You pushed it into the years when it costs the most.

What usually works better

Every year, take just enough from the account you haven't paid tax on to fill up the low tax brackets. Cover the rest of what you spend from your savings. Keep the Roth for last, or for a big one-time expense like a new roof or a car, when pulling from the untaxed account would push you into a higher bracket.

The number to know: in 2026, a married couple can have about $100,000 of taxable income, after deductions, and still be in the 12% bracket. Above that, the rate jumps to 22%. So in the years before required withdrawals start, every dollar you take out at 10% or 12% is a dollar you won't pay 22% or 24% on later.

An example: Bill and Susan

Bill and Susan are both 65. They have $200,000 in savings, $900,000 in accounts they haven't paid tax on, and $100,000 in a Roth. They spend about $90,000 a year.

The common way: they spend savings first. Their taxes are tiny for a few years, but the $900,000 keeps growing untouched. When required withdrawals start, those withdrawals plus Social Security push them into higher brackets for the rest of their lives, and it gets worse for whichever of them is left alone.

The other way: every year they take enough from the untaxed account to fill the 12% bracket and use savings for the rest. They pay a little more tax early, at a low rate. By the time required withdrawals start, the account is smaller, the forced withdrawals are smaller, and their tax bill stays flat instead of jumping. Over a full retirement, the difference can easily run into the tens of thousands.

Same money, same spending. The only thing that changed is the order.
