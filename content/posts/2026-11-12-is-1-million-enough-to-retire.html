For a lot of people, $1 million is the number. Get to a million and you can retire.

But a million dollars sounds a lot bigger than it is once you turn it into a paycheck. Here's what $1 million actually pays you, the three things that decide whether it's enough, and the one risk that can wreck it in the first five years.

Start with the math

The old rule of thumb is that you can take out about 4% of your money in the first year, raise that for inflation each year after, and it should last about 30 years.

4% of $1 million is $40,000 a year, about $3,300 a month, before taxes.

That's not all you live on. For a lot of married couples, Social Security between the two of you is somewhere around $3,000 to $4,000 a month. Add that in and you're closer to $7,000 a month before taxes.

Three things decide whether it's enough

1. What you actually spend

Not what you think you spend. Pull up your bank and credit card statements for the last 12 months and add it up. It's annoying, but most people are surprised, usually in the wrong direction. And the first few years of retirement are often the most expensive: that's when you travel, fix up the house and help the kids.

2. When you retire

If you retire before 65, you don't have Medicare yet, and health insurance for a couple can easily run over $1,000 a month until you get there. That comes straight out of your $40,000.

3. What kind of money it is

If your million is mostly in the plan from your old job, or an account you haven't paid tax on yet, it isn't really a million. Every dollar you take out gets taxed. Depending on your bracket, your million might be worth $800,000 or $850,000 to you.

Then there's inflation. At 3% a year, the $40,000 you need today becomes about $54,000 in ten years. Your paycheck has to grow just to stay even.

The risk that matters most: the first five years

Say you retire with $1 million and take out your $40,000. That first year, the market drops 20%. It happens; it happened in 2022.

Now you're down to about $760,000. To get back to $1 million from there, you don't need a 20% gain. You need about a 32% gain, while you keep taking money out every year.

That's why two people can retire with the same million dollars and the same investments, and one runs out while the other doesn't. One got a bad market in year one and had to sell low to pay the bills.

The fix: keep two to three years of withdrawals in safe money, like cash, high-quality bonds or something with a guarantee. When the market drops, you live off the safe money and leave the rest alone to recover. You never have to sell at the bottom. Protecting yourself from a big loss in those first years matters more than catching a big gain.

So, is $1 million enough?

For some people, absolutely. For others, not even close. It has a lot less to do with the number and a lot more to do with the plan around it.
