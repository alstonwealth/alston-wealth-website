Someone asked me recently: "Is $4 million enough to retire if I never eat out, never take a vacation, drive a 25-year-old car and buy everything used?"

On the numbers, it's not even close. Yes, easily. But that's not really what he was asking, and it's not the real risk for people like him.

The math first

The old 4% rule says you can take out about 4% of your savings the first year, then adjust for prices after that. On $4 million, that's about $160,000 a year, before Social Security. For a married couple, Social Security is often another $4,000 to $5,000 a month. So you're looking at $200,000 a year or more.

Here's what I usually see with savers like this: they're spending $60,000, maybe $80,000 a year, and the house is paid off. They could spend twice what they spend today and still be fine. More on what a smaller nest egg pays in is $1 million enough to retire.

Running out of money, or running out of time?

When I posted this question, one person replied with something I can't stop thinking about: is the greater risk running out of money, or running out of time?

For great savers, it's almost always time. The years you're healthy enough to travel hard, walk all day, and get on the floor with the grandkids are shorter than the years your money will last. Your money might last to 100. Your energy won't.

Why it's so hard to spend

  1. Habit. You spent 30 years saying no. That doesn't switch off the day you retire.
  2. Every dollar feels like a loss. When you were working, a paycheck refilled the account. Now nothing does, so every withdrawal feels like draining something.
  3. Fear of the next crash. You remember 2008. You remember 2000 to 2002. Part of you is still waiting for the floor to drop out.
  4. You don't have a number. This is the big one. Nobody has ever shown you, in writing, exactly how much you can spend this year without running out. So you guess low. Way low.

What guessing low costs

The trips get pushed back a year, then another year. You hold off on helping the kids. And the money you worked for ends up going to taxes and to heirs instead of the life you planned.

Four ways to fix it

1. Get a written spending number

Not a gut feeling. A plan that says you can spend this much every year, and shows how it holds up even if the market drops right after you retire. When you see that number in writing, it gets a lot easier to book the trip.

2. Set up your money so spending feels safe

Cover your must-pay bills with income that doesn't move with the market, like Social Security, a pension, or a guaranteed contract. Keep two to three years of withdrawals in safe money so a bad year doesn't scare you. Then give fun money its own line in the budget, so spending isn't breaking a rule. It is the rule.

3. Spend on purpose while you're healthy

Most people spend more in their 60s and early 70s and less after that. Plan for it: front-load the travel. If you want to help the kids or grandkids, think about doing it while you're here to see it. You can give up to $19,000 a year to as many people as you want without even filing a gift tax form.

4. Check it once a year

If the market had a good year, give yourself a raise. If it had a bad year, trim the extras. A plan that bends a little keeps you from spending too much in a bad year and too little every other year.

The takeaway

The goal of saving was never to die with a big balance. It was to have the life you saved for while you can still enjoy it. If you're a saver who can't flip the switch, you're not alone, and it's very fixable. If you'd like us to work out your number, a free call is the place to start.
