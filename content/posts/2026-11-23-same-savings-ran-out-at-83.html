Two people retire at 65. Same $800,000 saved. Same spending: $40,000 a year to start, going up a little each year with prices. Same investments, and over the next 30 years, the exact same average return.

One of them runs out of money at 83. The other is 95 and still has about $330,000 left.

Same savings, same spending, same returns. So what happened?

The only difference: when the bad years showed up

The first person retired right into a bad market. It dropped 20% in their first year and another 10% in their second. After that, it was steady, good years the rest of the way.

The second person got the exact same years, just in the opposite order. Good, steady years first, and the same two bad years at the very end, in their 90s.

Look where they are at 75, only 10 years in. The first person has about $364,000 left. The second has about $907,000. Same money, same choices, and one of them has more than double the other. The first person never catches back up and runs out at 83.

Why the order matters so much

When you're working and saving, a bad year doesn't hurt much. You're not touching the money. You keep adding to it, you're buying while prices are low, and you wait for it to come back.

In retirement, that flips. Money comes out every month, whether the market is up or down. When the market drops in your first years, you're selling to pay bills at the worst possible time. Each share is worth less, so you have to sell more of them to get the same $40,000.

Here's the part people miss: the shares you sold at the bottom are gone. When the market comes back, they don't come back with it. You only get the recovery on what you have left. It's not the drop that does the damage. It's having to sell during it.

The 10 years that matter most

Nobody can tell you when the next bad market is coming, and anyone who says they can is guessing. But we do know when it hurts the most: the five years before you retire and the five years after. That 10-year window is where a bad market can change the rest of your retirement. The same drop in your 80s does far less damage.

Three things I'd put in place before you retire

1. Keep two to three years of withdrawals in safe money

Cash, high-quality bonds, or something with a guarantee. That money has one job: to be there in a bad year. When the market drops, you live off the safe money and leave the rest alone to recover, so you're never forced to sell at the bottom. When the market comes back, you refill it.

2. Build a floor under your must-pay bills

Add up the bills that get paid no matter what: the mortgage or rent, food, utilities, insurance. Then cover as much of that as you can with income that doesn't move with the market, like Social Security, a pension, or a product with a guarantee. When the must-pay bills are covered, a bad market is an annoyance instead of an emergency. You're not lying awake wondering about the light bill.

3. Have a spending plan that can bend

In a bad year, skip the raise. Push the big trip back a year. Hold off on the new car. Small changes in a bad year make a big difference over 30 years, because you're selling less when prices are low, and that adds up.

What that would have done for the first person

If I'd sat down with the first person before they retired, those three things are what I'd want in place. The safe money gets them through the first two years without selling at the bottom. The floor keeps the bills paid. And a little flexibility in a bad year keeps more money invested for the recovery when it comes.
