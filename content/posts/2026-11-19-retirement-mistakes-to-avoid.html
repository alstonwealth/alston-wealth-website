I've spent about 10 years sitting across the table from families who are getting close to retirement. The same seven mistakes keep showing up. Not from people who didn't save, but from good savers: people who did almost everything right.

1. A pile, but no paycheck

Almost everyone knows their balance. Far fewer can tell me how much that balance pays them every month, and which account it comes from. Your balance isn't your retirement. Your paycheck is. You should be able to answer three questions: how much comes in every month, where it comes from, and how long it lasts.

2. Taking money out in the wrong order

The common advice is to spend your savings first, then the account you haven't paid tax on, and save the Roth for last. It feels smart, because your taxes are tiny for a few years. But the untaxed account keeps growing, and at 73 or 75 you're required to start pulling money out of it every year. Stack Social Security on top and your taxes jump right when you can least control them. Usually it works better to take a little from the untaxed account each year to fill the low tax brackets. More on that in which account to take money from first.

3. Claiming Social Security like you're one person

When one spouse passes away, the smaller Social Security check goes away and the survivor keeps the bigger one. So the higher earner's claiming age isn't just about them. It decides what the survivor lives on, maybe for decades. I see a lot of higher earners claim at 62 without thinking about that. Here's the math on 62 vs 70 for married couples.

4. Everything in one bucket

If all your money is in the market and the market drops 20% in your first year of retirement, you're selling low to pay the bills, and that early hit can follow you for the rest of your retirement. The fix is simple: keep two to three years of withdrawals in safe money, like cash, high-quality bonds or something with a guarantee. When the market drops, you live off that and leave the rest alone to recover. Protecting yourself from a big loss in the first years matters more than catching a big gain.

5. The health insurance gap and the Medicare deadline

Retire before 65 and you don't have Medicare yet. Health insurance for a couple can easily run over $1,000 a month until you get there, and a lot of people never put that in the budget. Then, at 65, there's a sign-up window. Miss it without other qualifying coverage and you can pay a penalty of 10% added to your premium for every full year you were late, for life.

6. Old beneficiary forms

Your will doesn't decide who gets your retirement accounts. The beneficiary form on each account does. If that form still names an ex-spouse, a parent who has passed away, or nobody at all, that's where the money goes, no matter what your will says. Pull every account and check who's listed. It takes about 20 minutes.

7. Planning for one retirement instead of two

Most couples plan for the two of them together. But one of you will very likely outlive the other, and when that happens, three things hit at once:

  • One Social Security check goes away.
  • The survivor files taxes as a single person, with tax brackets about half the size, so the tax bill often goes up even though income went down. People call it the widow's tax.
  • Very often, the person who handled all the money is the one who's gone.

The fix is to plan for both retirements: the one you'll have together and the one the survivor will have alone. Run the numbers both ways, and make sure both of you know where everything is.
